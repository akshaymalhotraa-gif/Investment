## Narrowbody takeaways for the RR thesis

1. **RR has zero share of the largest engine market in the world.** The A320neo + 737 MAX + A220 orderbooks total ~13,000 undelivered aircraft — none will carry an RR engine. RR's civil aerospace story is a pure widebody (plus business jet) story until at least the 2030s.
2. **The V2500 exit looks costly in hindsight.** RR sold its 32.5% IAE stake in 2012 for $1.5bn plus a 15-year flight-hour annuity that expires around 2027. The V2500 fleet (~5,500 engines in service) still has decades of high-margin shop visits ahead — economics now captured by P&W/MTU. The tail-off of these IAE payments is a modest headwind to watch in Civil Aerospace revenue around 2027.
3. **The 757/RB211 base is a nice, but melting, annuity.** ~60% RR share of a ~550-strong, freighter-heavy fleet; conversions extend life but retirements (United by end-2026) outpace additions.
4. **Optionality, not exposure: UltraFan.** RR's stated route back into narrowbody is UltraFan-derived engines for next-generation single-aisles (2030s). The GTF durability crisis and LEAP time-on-wing issues show both the size of the prize and the execution risk of this market.
5. **Cross-read:** the narrowbody rows explain why GE Aerospace/Safran and P&W trade on aftermarket annuity narratives — and why RR's equivalent annuity is the Trent widebody installed base in the table above.
